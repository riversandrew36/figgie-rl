How Figgie works

The deck has 40 cards: one suit has 12, two have 10, and one has 8. The goal suit is the other suit of the same color as the 12-card suit. Only goal cards pay at the end. Bots see their own hands and public market information; this viewer lets you inspect every hand.

A bid is an offer to buy; an ask is an offer to sell. Trade to collect valuable cards or earn cash from favorable prices.

Worked example: if spades has 12 cards and clubs has 10, clubs scores. Ten club cards pay $10 each, leaving $100 of the $200 pot. The player with the most clubs gets that $100; tied leaders split it. Starting with $350, posting a $50 ante, spending a net $20 on trades, and ending with six clubs and the sole majority gives $350 − $50 − $20 + $60 + $100 = $440, a $90 profit.

Rules and scripted baselines in this viewer:
  • Each bot starts with $350 and antes $50 (total pot $200).
  • All 40 cards are dealt evenly (10 each).
  • Bots trade during the selected timer length.
  • At settlement, each goal suit card pays $10 from the pot.
  • Bot(s) with most goal suit cards split the remaining pot.
  • Every bot estimates goal-suit odds from its starting hand, then plays its own style:
  • Hunter lifts offers early to corner its likely goal suit.
  • Banker quotes both sides around fair value and earns the spread.
  • Detective waits, reads who is paying up on the tape, then follows.
  • Bluffer buys a decoy suit to fake demand, sells into it, and accumulates quietly.

        FROM RULES TO LEARNED POLICIES

        Reinforcement learning is the experiment

        The browser bots follow hand-written rules. In the Python lab, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) learns a policy from repeated simulated rounds. The research question: can an agent learn profitable trading and inference beyond these baselines?

        1. Observe

        Inventory, cash, market quotes, own quotes, trade flow, and time remaining. Optional features add flow summaries and goal probabilities inferred from the initial private hand. The policy does not receive the hidden goal label.

        2. Act

        Hold, buy, sell, or quote bids and asks through a discrete action space. Python supports an outside-book market and a player-quote market.

        3. Learn from rewards

        Trade and inventory value changes provide feedback; settlement replaces marked inventory with its actual payoff. Checkpoints are selected by validation bankroll, not training reward alone.

        4. Test and repeat

        Compare PPO against hold, random, market-maker, Bayesian, and style baselines on paired episode seeds. Keep validation separate from final testing. Self-play introduces frozen learned opponents.

        Run your first RL experiment

        Clone the repository and use Python 3.10 or newer. Installation includes PyTorch and can take a few minutes. This small run checks the workflow; it is not evidence of strong play.

        python -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate
python -m pip install -e ".[dev]"
figgie-train --config configs/ppo_learning.yaml
figgie-eval --model-path artifacts/ppo_learning/best_model/best_model.zip \
  --config configs/ppo_learning.yaml --episodes 50 --seed 610000 \
  --benchmark --output results/learning.json

        Simulation assumptions and scope

        The browser uses a separate JavaScript simulation with drifting external quotes and probabilistic fills. It illustrates baseline behavior; it does not execute Python or trained PPO checkpoints. Python also offers a player-quote market with no outside book. Seeds, mechanics, and results are not interchangeable between the implementations.
